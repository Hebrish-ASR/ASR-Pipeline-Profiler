title: A short history of the bicycle

The bicycle is one of those inventions that seems obvious once it exists. Two wheels, a frame, a seat and some pedals. Yet it took most of the nineteenth century, and a great many strange experiments, before anyone settled on the shape we recognise today.

The earliest versions had no pedals at all. Riders sat on a wooden frame and pushed themselves along the road with their feet, a little like a child on a balance bike. They were fashionable for a few summers, and then quietly forgotten when people realised how tiring and uncomfortable they were.

Pedals arrived later, fixed directly to the front wheel. Because one turn of the pedals meant one turn of the wheel, the only way to go faster was to make the front wheel bigger. That is how we ended up with the famous high wheelers, with a front wheel taller than the rider and a tiny wheel at the back. They were fast, but a sudden stop could throw the rider straight over the handlebars.

The real change came with the chain. Once the pedals could drive the rear wheel through a set of gears, both wheels could be the same modest size, and the rider could sit low and safe between them. Air filled tyres followed soon after, and suddenly cycling was comfortable enough for ordinary people to use every day.

Within a few years, bicycles changed how people lived. Workers could live further from the factory, young people could travel to the next town on a Sunday afternoon, and many women found a new kind of independence on two wheels. Not bad for a machine that still looks almost exactly the same more than a century later.
